# Environmental Composition as Bounded Operational Autonomy
## From prescribed paths to authored possibility spaces
Environmental composition shifts part of the engineering task from specifying a sequence of operations to specifying the circumstances under which operations can become relevant. A GRAIL *world* declares conditions, affordances, prerequisites, effects, and bindings to operational capabilities. A *scenario* supplies a goal, initial worldstate, and inputs; *observations* preserve a record of attempted and completed activity. These three artifacts separate what may happen, what is sought, and what was reported to have happened. The distinction does not eliminate programming: authors must still design the world and implement capabilities. It instead changes what is fixed before execution. A workflow may prescribe a path, whereas a composed world may admit several paths that become distinguishable only during a pursuit. This is a contrast with path-prescriptive workflows, not with every workflow technology: declarative workflow systems and planning formalisms also allow runtime choice.

The value of composition therefore cannot rest on the novelty of preconditions and effects alone. STRIPS already represented actions through conditions and consequences [FikesNilsson1971]. GRAIL provides a concrete coordination architecture in which those declarations are paired with executable, independently implemented capabilities, two localized selection decisions, and inspectable pursuit observations. The claim examined here is modest: that this arrangement is sufficient to produce useful goal-directed behavior in several bounded software environments without requiring a general-purpose planner in the coordination runtime.

## Possibility, eligibility, and reported consequence
Three elements define the immediate decision space. *Possibility* concerns the affordances the author makes available: the runtime cannot select an action that the world does not declare. *Eligibility* concerns prerequisites and available inputs: a declared action may exist but not yet be invocable. *Consequence* concerns effects credited to a successful invocation: these change GRAIL's internal account of which responsibilities have been fulfilled. These are separable obligations. An affordance may be available in the registry but blocked by an unmet prerequisite; a capability may return an unfavorable business decision while successfully fulfilling its declared responsibility; and a capability may report success without producing a corresponding external change.

This last case is not a minor implementation detail. The internal state records what the runtime has reason to credit based on declared effects and reported invocation results. It does not constitute an independently verified description of the external domain. Experiment E5 makes this distinction observable: both scenarios reach the internal goal `recordCreated`, but only the truthful-success control produces an external record. Thus the environment's composition establishes a boundary for action and attribution, not a universal guarantee about external truth.

## Two local decisions: needs and means
GRAIL separates selection of an unmet condition—a *need*—from selection of an affordance capable of satisfying it—a *means*. This separation matters because a pursuit need not commit in advance to one complete invocation sequence. Different unmet conditions may be selected, and a selected condition may have multiple candidate producers. The runtime can also use feedback from a failed invocation to exclude a previously attempted affordance and choose an alternative. E1 records two such fallback events during one successful pursuit; E6 shows that failure exclusion can apply to a multi-effect affordance even when a different declared effect is subsequently needed.

We call this *bounded operational autonomy*: authority to choose intermediate needs and means, and to respond to execution feedback, within an authored environment. Autonomy here refers to consequential delegated choice, not to the intelligence, optimality, or novelty of the selection policy. The inspected implementation uses randomized choice among eligible candidates with failure-based filtering; it does not implement a general-purpose planning algorithm or demonstrate adaptive learning. A choice can be operationally meaningful even when the selection rule is simple. Conversely, meaningful choice does not imply that the rule is efficient or that it will terminate on every world. E4's cyclic prerequisites caused repeated resolution attempts until an external timeout, illustrating a structural boundary of this autonomy.

## Intellectual precedents and the location of complexity
This account draws on, rather than displaces, earlier work. Simon's bounded rationality locates decisions within limits on available alternatives and information [Simon1955]; his analysis of complex systems motivates attention to modular organization [Simon1962]. The relevance to GRAIL is architectural: much of the coordination problem is constrained before runtime by the environment author. This does not show that the selectors themselves implement Simon's theory of satisficing. Gibson's notion of affordances identifies action possibilities relative to an environment [Gibson1979], while Norman emphasizes the design and legibility of possible actions [Norman1988]. GRAIL's affordances are explicit software declarations, not ecological affordances perceived directly in a physical world.

Maes' work on situated agents with goals offers a closer precedent for goal-directed action selection through local arbitration rather than complete advance planning [Maes1990]. Its activation-based mechanism differs from GRAIL's current selectors, so the comparison supports a family resemblance rather than algorithmic equivalence. Brooks argues against assuming that useful autonomous behavior always requires a comprehensive centralized representation [Brooks1991]; GRAIL nevertheless maintains an explicit symbolic worldstate and action model, and therefore should not be described as representation-free. These connections suggest a shared design question: how much behavior can be obtained by structuring the agent--environment relationship rather than expanding a centralized reasoning mechanism?

The environment also relocates complexity rather than eliminating it. The author must select appropriate responsibilities, encode dependencies, provide alternatives, define bindings, and decide which claims can be trusted. Capabilities retain the work of interpreting domain outcomes, including policies, credentials, human decisions, and external protocols. E2 demonstrates that a parent pursuit can delegate verification to a child world while treating a `REJECTED` verification result as a completed responsibility. E3 shows a single purchasing world coordinating HTTP, Node, and interactive STDIO across `approved`, `rejected`, and `deferred` decisions without making those domain decisions inside the runtime.

## Architectural economy as a research motivation
An important practical motivation is *architectural economy*: using only as much centralized reasoning machinery as routine coordination requires. In the studied worlds, GRAIL's coordination mechanism does not require an LLM. This is a meaningful feasibility result, because it separates goal-directed execution from a dependency on language-model inference. It is not evidence that LLMs are unnecessary in every capability; a capability may use a language model, conventional domain logic, or human judgment while remaining behind the same operational boundary.

Nor do the present experiments establish cost-effectiveness in a comparative economic sense. They contain no controlled measurements of engineering effort, inference expenditure, operating cost, or maintenance burden against alternative architectures. A lightweight coordinator may reduce *runtime coordination cost* while increasing *environment composition cost*: authoring and validating affordances, capabilities, and dependencies can be substantial work. The defensible proposition is therefore that environmental composition *offers a potentially economical route* to bounded agentic solutions, not that it has been shown cheaper or more scalable than workflow engines, planners, or LLM-based agents.

The experiments support a narrower but consequential conclusion. E1--E3 demonstrate autonomous selection, delegated execution, recursive composition, and separation of business outcome from responsibility completion in authored worlds. E4--E6 identify limitations in termination, external verification, and failure-memory scope. Taken together, they indicate that bounded operational autonomy can be achieved through local need-and-means selection over composed possibilities, while the quality and guarantees of that autonomy remain constrained by the environment and the capability trust boundary.
